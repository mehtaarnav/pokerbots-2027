Kuhn poker, solved in your browser

A small, correctness step from the same project: before scaling a solver up to a real poker variant, it has to be checked against a game small enough to solve exactly by hand. This page runs that check live. No server, no precomputed answers shipped to the page — the algorithm below runs in this tab and arrives at the known solution itself.

The game

Kuhn poker is a three-card poker game introduced by Harold Kuhn in 1950, used ever since as the standard toy example for testing equilibrium solvers. Two players each ante one chip and are dealt one card from a deck of three (J, Q, K). Player one may check or bet one chip; the other player may then check, bet, call, or fold, in the combinations a single betting round allows. Highest card wins at showdown.

It is small enough — twelve decision points total — that its exact equilibrium strategy and exact game value are known analytically. That makes it the right first target for validating a solver: if the algorithm cannot find this answer, it has no business being pointed at a game nobody can solve by hand.

Solving it

Counterfactual regret minimization: repeated self-play where each player tracks how much they regret not having taken each action more often, and shifts their strategy toward actions with positive regret. Averaged over enough iterations, this converges to a Nash equilibrium.

Play it

Play a few hands against the strategy this page just derived. Kuhn poker's equilibrium is asymmetric: even played perfectly, the player who acts first is at a small structural disadvantage, worth exactly 1/18 of a chip per hand on average. That number only means something once you've watched a solver produce it independently — which is what the panel above just did.
